# Progressive Lenses

Progressive lenses, also called progressive addition lenses (PAL) or "no-line bifocals", are multifocal lenses that correct vision at every distance in one lens:

- The top of the lens is for distance vision (driving, walking, watching TV).
- The middle corridor is for intermediate distance (computer screens, a car dashboard).
- The bottom of the lens is for near vision (reading, a phone).

The power changes gradually from top to bottom, so there is no visible line on the lens.

## Who they are for

Progressive lenses are for people with presbyopia, the normal age-related loss of near focusing ability that usually starts around age 40. A prescription for progressive lenses includes an ADD (addition) value.

## Getting used to progressive lenses

Most people adapt to progressive lenses within 1 to 2 weeks. Tips for adapting:

- Wear the new glasses consistently all day rather than switching back to old glasses.
- Point your nose at what you want to see: turn your head rather than only moving your eyes sideways.
- To read, lower your eyes, not your head.
- Take extra care on stairs at first. Tilt your head down slightly so you look through the distance part of the lens.

Some soft blur at the lower sides of the lens is normal. It is a trade-off of every progressive design. Premium "freeform" designs have wider clear zones and less side blur.

## Frame requirements

The frame must be tall enough to fit all the zones. As a general guide, the lens height should be at least 28 to 30 mm, depending on the lens design. Very narrow frames make the reading area smaller. Accurate fitting measurements are essential: the monocular pupillary distance (PD for each eye) and the fitting height are measured in store with the chosen frame on the customer's face.

## Progressive vs. bifocal

Progressive lenses have no visible line and include an intermediate zone. Bifocal lenses have a wider, more stable reading area but a visible line and no intermediate zone. For people who spend long hours at a desk, dedicated office or computer lenses are an alternative, with a larger intermediate zone.
